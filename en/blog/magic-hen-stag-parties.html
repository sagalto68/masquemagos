Magic for hen and stag parties: the different touch

September 2026 · 7 min read

How to surprise the group with something other than the usual. If you've ended up organising it, you already know the hard part isn't booking the restaurant: it's finding something the bride or groom will actually enjoy, that works with a group where not everyone knows each other, and that isn't exactly what you did at the last one you went to.

In this article:

  • When to hold the party
  • Who pays and how to handle the budget
  • What to do beyond eating and going out
  • Why workshops work so well
  • How magic fits in (and in which format)

When should a hen or stag party be held?

Most are held between one and two months before the wedding. Leaving it to the week before usually backfires: it clashes with the busiest days of preparations, the final fittings and the nerves, and leaves no recovery time. Holding it too early doesn't work either, because it loses that countdown feeling which is exactly what's being celebrated.

If the group is large or people are travelling in, it's worth locking the date well in advance and telling everyone as early as possible — coordinating diaries is almost always what delays the organising most.

Who pays?

The most widespread rule in Spain is clear: the person being celebrated pays nothing. The cost is split between those attending, and whoever organises usually fronts the bookings.

Two pieces of advice that prevent most of the arguments in the WhatsApp group:

  • Set the price per person before booking anything. Better to find out the budget doesn't add up before you've paid a deposit.
  • Collect the money upfront. Whoever organises shouldn't end up out of pocket because three people forgot to transfer their share.

The detail everyone forgets: ask what the person being celebrated does NOT want. If they hate costumes, sashes or being put on the spot in front of people, spending money on that is wasting it — and ruining the day for the one person it's supposed to be for.

What to do beyond eating and going out

The classic formula — lunch, forfeit games, a long night — works, but it has a known problem: it depends on the whole group being equally up for it at the same time. And almost every party includes people who don't know each other: the cousin, the colleague from work, the university friend who only knows two others there.

That's why more and more groups add one concrete activity somewhere in the day — a magic show or workshop, for example. Not to replace the meal or the night out, but to have a stretch with content of its own that breaks the ice before the rest kicks off.

Why workshops have become so popular

Ceramics, cocktail making, cooking, painting… group workshops have become one of the most common hen and stag plans, and there's a practical reason behind it: they give people something to do, not just something to watch. Nobody has to force conversation, because there's a shared activity occupying hands and attention. And everyone goes home with something.

The downside of some of these workshops is that they're quiet or very individual — each person focused on their own piece. If you're after something with more interaction across the group and more laughs, that's where a magic workshop fits well.

How magic fits into a hen or stag party

There are three formats, and they work very differently:

1. A magic show for the whole group

A show designed so everyone looks in the same direction for a while, with the group seated or standing around. It's the format with the biggest moments, and the one people talk about most afterwards.

It fits particularly well if you rent a country house — one of the most common weekend hen and stag plans: you can book the show for after lunch or dinner, when the group is already together and relaxed around the table, with no need to travel anywhere. It fills exactly that gap where the afternoon or evening tends to stall before the next thing starts.

2. A magic workshop for the whole group

Here you don't watch magic: you learn to do it. In a couple of hours everyone leaves knowing several complete tricks, with the props to repeat them later. It's the "creative workshop" version of the day, but with the advantage that people interact constantly instead of each being absorbed in their own lump of clay.

It works especially well when the group spans very different ages or includes people who've never met, because everyone starts from zero equally — there's nobody who's done it before and nobody who hasn't.

3. Close-up magic during the meal

The magician comes over to the group with cards, coins or personal objects, a metre away. No stage needed, and no need for everyone to go quiet: it works in a private dining room, a country house or on a terrace. It's the easiest option to slot into a plan you've already locked in, because it takes up a gap in the day without changing anything else.

When in the day should it go? Early on, almost always. With the whole group present, still fresh, and before the day scatters into other activities. Saving it for the end risks half a dozen people already being elsewhere.

The detail that turns it into the memory of the day

What separates a good show from the memory of the whole party is that it has something to do with the person being celebrated. The partner's name, the wedding date, an inside joke only the group knows, or a photo you've sent over beforehand can all be built into the tricks. The final reveal stops being just another card and becomes something that only makes sense to you.

What to keep in mind when organising it

  • The venue matters less than you'd think. Close-up magic needs no setup, so a restaurant, a country house or an apartment all work. The only thing to avoid is a venue with music so loud you can't hear each other.
  • Tell us the real group size. Eight people and thirty are not the same: it changes the format, the duration and how it's organised.
  • Surprise or not, it works either way. Many groups keep it secret, but announcing it also helps: the anticipation does part of the work.
  • It works for joint parties. If you're celebrating both together, it's one of the few activities that doesn't need adapting depending on who turns up.
